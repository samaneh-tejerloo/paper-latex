

\section{Results and Discussion}

In this section, we conduct a series of experiments to evaluate the proposed approach for functional module identification in PPI networks. We first investigate the effects of different loss functions and GNN architectures to identify the most suitable model configuration. Based on the results of this comparison, we further examine the aggregation mechanisms of JKNet and conduct an ablation study to evaluate the effect of different activation functions on model performance. The experimental analysis is organized into the following stages:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Comparing the performance of different loss functions and GNN architectures
	\item Analyzing the effect of different JKNet aggregation mechanisms
	\item Comparing the performance of different activation functions
\end{itemize}

These experiments are conducted on three PPI datasets: Collins, Krogan-Core, and DIP, which are used for model architecture selection. BioGRID and Krogan-Extended are excluded from this stage and are subsequently used in the final comparison with existing methods. The improvements observed in the final results on these additional datasets provide further evidence supporting the effectiveness of the selected architecture and its suitability for functional module identification. In all experiments, binary GO features are used as input features from MF and BP subgraphs, and a threshold of $0.3$ is applied to the affiliation matrix to identify functional modules. The models are trained for 2,000 epochs on the Collins and Krogan-Core datasets and 5,000 epochs on the DIP dataset to accommodate the differences in dataset size.

Following these experiments, we evaluate the proposed threshold aggregation strategy for extracting functional modules from the affiliation matrix. Finally, we compare the performance of the selected model with existing methods from the literature to assess its effectiveness relative to previously proposed approaches.

\subsection{Comparing the performance of loss functions and graph neural network architecture}

In this experiment, we compare the proposed loss functions, W-NOCD and HO-NOCD, with the original NOCD loss using GCN and GAT architectures. The evaluation is conducted on the Collins, Krogan-Core, and DIP datasets, selected to represent different PPI network sizes and characteristics. Binary GO annotations derived from the BP and MF subgraphs are used as input features, and a fixed threshold of $0.3$ is applied to the learned protein–community membership matrix to identify functional modules. The results are presented in Table~\ref{table:loss_arch_results}.

Overall, W-NOCD combined with GAT achieves the highest F1-score across all evaluated datasets. W-NOCD also consistently improves F1-score over the original NOCD loss when used with GCN, highlighting the benefits of incorporating interaction weights into the loss function. In contrast, HO-NOCD does not consistently outperform the original loss, and increasing $\lambda$ generally leads to poorer performance. Although some configurations yield modest improvements, the higher-order formulation provides less consistent benefits than W-NOCD under the settings considered.

The precision and recall results further demonstrate the favorable balance achieved by W-NOCD, particularly when combined with GAT. Although accuracy varies across configurations, the consistent superiority in F1-score supports selecting GAT with W-NOCD as the final model. To further investigate the choice of GNN architecture, we also evaluate GATv2 and SuperGAT using binary GO annotations. As reported in Appendix~\ref{}, neither variant outperforms the standard GAT, further supporting its selection for functional module identification.

\subsection{Analyzing the JKNet Mechanism}

To investigate the effect of layer aggregation on functional module identification, we evaluate MaxPooling, concatenation, and LSTM-based attention using binary GO features, the W-NOCD loss, and GAT as the base GNN. Experiments are conducted on the Collins, Krogan-Core, and DIP datasets, with results reported in Table~\ref{table:jknet_binary}.

The results show that concatenation achieves the highest F1-score on Collins and DIP, while LSTM-based attention performs best on Krogan-Core by a small margin. Although the best-performing strategy varies across datasets, concatenation generally provides a favorable balance between precision and recall. MaxPooling does not achieve the highest F1-score on any dataset, while the differences among aggregation strategies are relatively small on Krogan-Core and DIP.

Overall, concatenation is a competitive aggregation mechanism, particularly on Collins, where its advantage is more pronounced. However, none of the JKNet strategies consistently outperforms the standard GAT architecture evaluated in the preceding experiments. This suggests that aggregating representations from multiple layers does not necessarily improve functional module identification in the considered settings.

\subsection{Comparing the Activation Functions}

In this experiment, we investigate the effect of ReLU, ELU, and GELU activation functions on functional module identification using binary GO annotations and GO2Vec embeddings across five PPI datasets. The results in Table~\ref{table:activation_function_comparison} illustrate how activation function performance varies with the input representation and dataset characteristics.

For binary GO features, ReLU achieves the highest F1-score on most datasets, while GELU performs best on Krogan-Core and Krogan-Extended. Similarly, ReLU generally provides higher recall, whereas ELU tends to favor precision at the expense of recall. When GO2Vec embeddings are used, ReLU achieves the highest F1-score on four datasets and consistently yields the highest recall, while ELU performs best on Collins. These findings indicate that ReLU offers a more favorable balance between precision and recall across different datasets and feature representations.

Overall, binary GO features generally outperform GO2Vec embeddings in terms of F1-score, particularly when combined with ReLU. Although ELU and GELU occasionally achieve higher precision or accuracy, these improvements do not consistently translate into better overall performance. Therefore, ReLU is selected for subsequent experiments due to its consistently strong F1-score and recall across the evaluated datasets.

\subsection{Aggregated Thresholding and Input Features}

This subsection investigates the effects of input feature representation and threshold selection on the performance of the proposed method across all five PPI datasets. Specifically, we examine the contribution of binary GO features and GO2Vec embeddings, both individually and in combination, under two thresholding strategies: a fixed threshold of $0.3$ and the proposed aggregated thresholding mechanism. The results are presented in Table~\ref{table:merge-thresholds-results}, providing an ablation analysis of the input features and thresholding strategy.

The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed aggregated thresholding strategy across all five datasets and different feature configurations. Compared with using a fixed threshold, aggregation consistently improves recall and F1-score, regardless of the input feature representation. This indicates that combining predictions obtained at different thresholds enables the model to identify a broader range of functional modules, including those that might be missed when relying on a single threshold. Although this improvement is generally accompanied by a slight reduction in precision, the overall increase in F1-score suggests a more favorable balance between precision and recall. Furthermore, accuracy also improves across all datasets and feature configurations, providing additional evidence of the benefits of the aggregation strategy.

Regarding input feature representations, combining binary GO features with GO2Vec embeddings consistently achieves the highest F1-score among the three feature configurations across all five datasets, under both thresholding strategies. This observation highlights the complementary nature of these representations. While binary GO features encode the presence or absence of functional annotations, GO2Vec embeddings capture semantic relationships between GO terms. Their combination therefore provides the model with complementary functional information, leading to more effective identification of protein functional modules. Although individual feature representations may achieve higher precision in some cases, their combination provides a better overall balance between precision and recall.

Finally, the combination of binary GO features and GO2Vec embeddings with the aggregated thresholding strategy achieves the best F1-score across all five datasets. This finding demonstrates that the benefits of integrating complementary biological features are further enhanced by the proposed threshold aggregation mechanism. In particular, the combined strategy improves the identification of functional modules while maintaining a favorable balance between precision and recall. Based on these observations, we adopt the combination of binary GO features and GO2Vec embeddings together with the aggregated thresholding strategy as the final configuration of our proposed method.

\subsection{Comparing to Other Methods}

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method, we compare its performance with several existing approaches for protein functional module identification across five PPI datasets. The comparison includes traditional clustering algorithms, such as MCODE, MCL, ClusterOne, CMC, and COACH, as well as methods based on network representation learning and graph neural networks, including TADW-SC, GANE, DPCMNE, R-GMM-VGAE, Protein-AGC, and AdaPPI. For a fair comparison, the baseline results are adopted from AdaPPI~\cite{adappi}, and the results of the proposed method are obtained using the selected GAT architecture, W-NOCD loss, combined binary GO and GO2Vec features, and the proposed aggregated thresholding strategy. The results are presented in Table~\ref{table:results-comparison}.

As shown in the table, the proposed method achieves the highest F1-score across all five datasets, demonstrating its consistent performance in identifying functional modules. On the Collins dataset, our method obtains an F1-score higher than that of AdaPPI, which achieves the second-best performance. A similar improvement is observed on Krogan-Core, where the proposed method slightly outperforms AdaPPI. The performance gain is more noticeable on Krogan-Extended and DIP, where our method achieves higher F1-scores than all the compared approaches. On BioGRID, the proposed method also achieves the best F1-score, slightly surpassing GANE, which provides the second-best result. These findings indicate that the proposed framework maintains its effectiveness across PPI networks of different sizes and characteristics.

Considering the individual evaluation metrics, the proposed method also achieves the highest recall on Krogan-Core, Krogan-Extended, and DIP, indicating its ability to identify a larger proportion of reference functional modules. Although some competing methods obtain higher precision on individual datasets, our method provides a more favorable balance between precision and recall, resulting in consistently higher F1-scores. Furthermore, the proposed method achieves the highest accuracy on Collins and DIP, while its accuracy remains competitive on the other datasets. Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of integrating GO-based functional information with PPI network structure through a graph attention architecture and a weighted overlapping-community detection objective. The consistent improvements in F1-score over both conventional clustering approaches and more recent learning-based methods support the suitability of the proposed framework for functional module identification in PPI networks.

\subsection{Statistical and Structural Analysis of Predicted Modules}


In this subsection, we examine the protein modules identified by the proposed method and assess how well they capture the characteristics of the gold-standard modules. We first compare the statistical properties of the predicted and reference modules across the datasets, as reported in Table~\ref{table:predicted-modules-size-stats}.

The results indicate that the predicted modules generally exhibit lower variability in their sizes than the reference modules. In particular, the standard deviation of module sizes is consistently lower for the predicted modules across all datasets. Their maximum sizes are also considerably smaller. However, these differences should be interpreted in the context of the underlying module-size distributions. In the reference sets, very large modules are relatively rare, while most modules fall within the small-to-medium size range. Consequently, the maximum size and standard deviation can be strongly influenced by a small number of exceptionally large modules. Therefore, comparing the complete distributions of module sizes, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:complexe-sizes-vilon-plot}, provides a more comprehensive assessment of these differences.

\begin{figure*}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/complexes_vilon_plot.jpg}
	\label{fig:complexe-sizes-vilon-plot}
	\caption{Comparison of the size distributions of predicted functional modules and gold-standard modules}
\end{figure*}

We also examine the extent of overlap among modules by measuring the percentage of proteins assigned to more than one module. This percentage ranges from 78.92\% to 83.36\% in the reference sets and from 75.20\% to 84.42\% in the predicted sets. These results indicate that a substantial proportion of proteins participate in multiple modules, consistent with the inherently overlapping organization of protein complexes and functional modules. Although the predicted modules exhibit a lower overlap percentage in some datasets, in others, this value is comparable to or slightly higher than that of the reference sets. This suggests that the proposed method captures a considerable degree of the overlapping structure present in the reference modules.

To further investigate the effectiveness of the aggregated threshold and its impact on recovering nested module pairs, we compare the proposed method using a fixed threshold of 0.3 with its aggregated-threshold strategy. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:nested-complex-recovery}, the aggregated threshold consistently achieves better recovery of nested module pairs across all datasets. The fixed threshold of 0.3 is selected for this comparison because it demonstrated relatively better performance among the evaluated fixed-threshold values. These findings suggest that aggregating predictions across multiple thresholds improves the ability of the proposed method to recover nested relationships between modules.

An example of nested modules predicted in the Biogrid dataset is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:nested_complexes}. Although the protein YHR005C-A is not predicted as a member of either of the two corresponding modules, the predicted modules are still considered equivalent under the adopted criterion because their neighborhood similarity exceeds 0.25. This example illustrates how the proposed approach can identify nested module relationships even when the predicted protein memberships do not perfectly match those of the reference modules.

Overall, the statistical and structural analyses demonstrate that the proposed method identifies modules with reasonable size distributions while preserving a substantial degree of the overlapping organization observed in the reference sets. Furthermore, the comparison of thresholding strategies highlights the advantage of the aggregated threshold over a fixed threshold in recovering nested module relationships. The qualitative example also illustrates the method's ability to preserve such relationships in cases where the underlying reference modules exhibit nested structures. Taken together, these findings provide further evidence of the proposed method's ability to capture complex and overlapping modular structures in protein-protein interaction networks.
